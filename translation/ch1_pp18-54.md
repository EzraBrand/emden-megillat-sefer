Immediately after his wedding with my mother and teacher, the rebbetzin, of blessed memory, they gave him a
partner who understood trade, to make a profit with it, so that he could be diligent in his study in the house
of study while the partner did business with the money on his behalf. But the days were not long: his partner
fled from here to Amsterdam after the money had been eaten up, and my father was suddenly, in an instant, left
with nothing of his property, for all his money had been in the fugitive's hands, and nothing at all was left
to him. Over this the worry grew so great in his heart that he fell into the black illness [melancholy], out of
worry that he would need the help of other people. His cure became very hard; all the doctors in his town had
already given up on him, and they advised him to go to a place of a spring of living water, called the bath of
Ems, near the river Rhine. He did so: he travelled there to take the cure of the waters of that hot spring,
between Passover and Shavuot, at great expense. By the Lord's mercy it helped him, so that he found balm for his
illness through it, and he returned to health.

He also held a written guarantee and security from the fugitive's father, who was subject to the community of
Altona. It happened that this man fell ill and took to his bed, and his illness was very severe. My father
worried that perhaps it was because of the trouble his son had brought upon him ⟨S: "the debts"⟩ that the man
had fallen ill to the point of death. So my father took the bills of debt and sent them to him torn up, so that
he would not grieve or take to heart the matter of the flight; and the man recovered.

In the middle of Sivan in that period ⟨S: "the year [5]457 [1697]"⟩ I, Ya'avetz, was born, his first son. He
called me by his father's name, Jacob, of blessed memory; but I sign myself Ya'avetz, by my father's
instruction, as mentioned in the introduction to my book *She'ilat Ya'avetz* **[a]**. Three daughters had come
before me, and he too was in great sorrow, for he had almost given up hope of having a male child — thinking
that "three times make a presumption". He had already ordered and arranged another *mohel* in his place, if a
son should be born to him while he was away from home. But he came back home, whole in body and in health, a
day or two before the circumcision, and he brought me into the covenant and circumcised me himself, and it was a
great joy to him. I look to the mercy of Heaven that his hope and joy at the time of my birth may be fulfilled
in me; may it be His will that I shall not be ashamed or disgraced before my fathers.

And for all that his capital was spent and his money lost, he did not change his way: he made do with little,
with great frugality, against his nature and habit, and he bore much sorrow over his livelihood. But there were
then in the holy communities of Altona and Hamburg men of heart, generous men who "stand by generous things"

The well-known men who did business for him in this way were sons of distinguished and famous families in the
holy community of Altona. First of all, the head and first in every matter of holiness, the leader, learned
and outstanding in Torah and wisdom, the parnas and leader, the aged R. Moses, called R. Lipsch, who with his
fathers was among the founders of the holy community of Altona. This man Moses was very great in all the land of
Germany, the glory of his time; he left none like him among all the leaders of his generation. He had studied in
the yeshiva of the great and famous gaon R. Heschel, of blessed memory, when the land of Poland was in its
tranquillity. This important man understood and recognised at once my father's standing ⟨S: a note, not read⟩,
when he came to live in Altona after he married my mother the rebbetzin, as mentioned. My grandfather was then
already very old and heavy with years, while my father was still young at that time. This honoured parnas and
leader wanted the whole business of the rabbinate to be handed to the son-in-law of the rabbi and head of the
court, that is, my father, since the burden had become too heavy for my grandfather; the old head of the court
would keep the salary of the rabbinate as before, and a salary of its own would be fixed by the community for my
father, his son-in-law, for his service in ruling and in leading the community. But he did not succeed, because
of the accusers who are always to be found, who oppose what is right; and there were then men of a certain
family who leaned to another side, as I will write below with God's help.

The said magnate did, however, exert himself to set up a yeshiva for my father in Altona, in which my father
spread Torah with great diligence, and from which rulings went forth. All the notable public affairs also rested
on him, as mentioned, without any benefit or fee. Even so his livelihood came flying to him like a bird, through
important, beloved and very generous men — though they gave up nothing to him from their own purses. They were not
allowed to, even if they had wanted to make him a gift of their own in a generous spirit: he firmly prevented
them. So they could not benefit him except by making a profit with his money in good faith, as said. And this
kindness they did him: if some business came their way in which they saw profit and good reward to come, they
left it to him and gave up their own gain

a close family connection with my father, also did him kindness of this kind. As I was told, this leader set
aside a certain sum of his money for the dowry of my father's first daughter, my sister, of blessed memory. In
this way my father earned enough for his livelihood and the needs of his household with ease, without accepting
a present from any man.

The magnate, the parnas and leader R. Moses, mentioned above, also did this for him: he took out of his own
purse some thousands of Reichstaler to bring kosher wine from France and Italy, and he entreated my father to
take this in hand and to make an order and arrangement that the wine be made kosher according to halakhah, by
men sent there from here; and all the profit and the fruits of this business would be my father's. So he did,
and kept up this business for several years. It was both a commandment and a great profit, for he gave the
public the merit of kosher and choice wine, the like of which never came here again after him; and he saw in it
a good sign and a good reward for his toil.

Once he lost much of the wine because of the soldiers of the Swedish army. It was then a time of emergency and of
war between kingdoms, and my father was forced to move his dwelling from Altona and live in Hamburg with all he
had, until the fury of the men of war should pass. They "made" him wine worth eight hundred Reichstaler [i.e.
they touched it and made it forbidden], and he ordered all the wine poured out and the barrels broken that had
become forbidden by their touch; he would not sell them to a gentile and benefit from the money.

With all this, his "eye-salve" lacked nothing, so that he made his living with honour, without needing flesh and
blood at all — although his salary from the house of study of the Klaus, where he was head of the yeshiva as
mentioned, was only a small thing, sixty Reichstaler a year, while he needed at least a thousand Reichstaler a
year without any extravagance, for he was burdened with children. But all the time that his honour dwelt here,
blessing was to be found: the profit of the trade of the members of the community was great, the work of their
hands was blessed and the rich grew in number, and the communities kept growing in wealth and honour in his days
— silver was truly of no account — and every day the wealth of the Three Communities grew beyond all the holy
communities in Germany, until my father moved his dwelling from there. Then the people of the Lord went down
into troubles, as I will write. Surely his merit, and the merit of his study of Torah for its own sake and of his
holy conduct, drew down abundance, "satisfied with favour and full of the blessing of the Lord" (Deut. 33:23), in
this place, all the time that his honour dwelt here. So too everyone who was worthy to serve him became rich —
above all two attendants: one, Anshel Schuster in Altona, who was his paid servant, became rich with several
thousands; and R. A. N., who served him for nothing, rose to the heights of wealth

So he was loved by all the people, from end to end, though he was feared and held in awe, and the dread of him
lay on them as of a holy man of God — except for one powerful man in the holy community of Altona: the famous
leader R. Ber Kohen, of blessed memory **[a]**. This man was almost worshipped by most of the people of Altona
because of his great wealth and great success. For he had come here wandering for bread, a poor and needy young
man, and afterwards he became exceedingly rich. He also had no children until he was past fifty, and someone else
had already been [named](?) as heir to his great wealth. After his first wife died he married a young woman and had
a son by her; then she too died, and he married her sister and had sons and daughters, and he saw many joys in his
sons and daughters. He made great matches: his eldest son took the daughter of the great leader of Vienna, the
famous R. Samson [Wertheimer], of blessed memory.

That man prospered greatly in his old age: wealth, honour and greatness he had in huge abundance, the like of which
was not heard or seen in these generations. All the members of the community bowed to him and obeyed his word —
all the more since he was something of a scholar too. In truth he also had good habits: he was diligent at his book
whenever he was free from business; he set up a house of study in his home and supplied the needs of its students
amply and with great honour; and charity and kindness went out from his house to the poor of the city. But the
main benefactions came through his wives, above all the last: she did much good without his knowledge. For in truth
he was neither open-handed nor generous by nature; only his great wealth forced him not to be a miser. So, it
seems, he did not serve as he should have, in keeping with his great standing.

He was also old and held in high regard by the authorities. Therefore, willingly or unwillingly, the people of
Altona submitted to him and feared him greatly; for in his hands lay not only the leadership of the community but
also the standing of private persons: the merchants and traders who bought on credit were in his power. Whoever he
looked on with an evil eye, he cut off his livelihood, even if he stood well with great merchants and moneyed men
who gave credit; not to speak of the tax assessment — whom he wished he burdened, and whom he wished he eased:
"whom he would he raised up, and whom he would he put down" (Dan. 5:19), bringing a man down from his greatness and

in his refusal. They denied it to his face, as if it had never been: they had only sent him, they said, to entreat

what they wanted R. Joel Schwab to say, to excuse them. For they were very much afraid of being burned by my
father's coals: several times things had happened in which no man who opened his mouth and tongue against him went
unpunished, for the Holy One, blessed be He, demands His honour, and punished and killed. So when they saw that they
had made no fruit of their mission, they went back on it and regretted the deed — such a thing ought not to be done
in Israel, to remove from his office so holy a man and so great a sage, whose like would not be found in his
generation — and they feared for themselves that it would become a disgrace before their enemies. They would rather
my father had not done such a thing, to leave his place of honour suddenly. So they wanted the messengers to deny
the words of the mission, to cover for them, so that this stumbling-block should not be laid at their door.

Eli Falk, one of the messengers, did so, and flatly contradicted his colleague who had gone with him on the
community's errand to my father. But Joel Schwab, as a man of truth, stood by his words: he had changed nothing; he
had done what they commanded him and kept to the words they had put in his mouth, without turning to the right or
the left; he had added nothing and taken nothing away, good or bad. For this they fined Joel Schwab six hundred
Reichstaler, which he had to pay at once into the community chest; and he did so gladly. Besides this they gave him a
bitter punishment: not only was he put out of the community chamber, it was decreed that he be barred from any
office and never again come to any matter of holiness all his days, and that he sit in the synagogue with the
mourners and the excommunicated. So he sat as a mourner and an excommunicate for several years. The leader bore it
all and accepted it for the love of truth; and all the days of R. Ber Kohen's life Joel Schwab never again came to
any office in community affairs. The story is long; it remained in writing with R. Joel Schwab and was kept in his
archive as a memorial.

After this my father fell very seriously ill; they almost despaired of his recovery and had to let his blood. Then
his spirit came back to him, for he saw that the affair had been the cause.

Let us return to my father's story. Not half a year passed after the affair mentioned, in which he gave up his
honour and withdrew from the rabbinate of Altona, before a letter of appointment to the rabbinate came to him from
the community of Amsterdam: "He withdraws not his eyes from the righteous" (Job 36:7). It was on the eve of the
Sabbath of the portion *Beshallaḥ*; he was sitting in the synagogue wrapped in his tallit, reading the weekly portion
twice with the Targum — the portion of the Song — from a Torah scroll, when the letter was brought to him with the
good news.

But he wrote them an answer, and told them that unless they added to their salary for the rabbinate ⟨S: a five-line
note, partly read: "which was fixed … and they added for him … to receive the rabbinate"⟩ he would not stand and serve
before them. For they had formerly given their rabbi a small thing, about five hundred gulden only, and the main
support of their rabbi and head of the court was "a staff of reed": gifts and presents from householders. He did not
accept gifts from flesh and blood at all, as said; his household was large, and living in a great city like
Amsterdam is hard; so of necessity they must add for him from the community chest, a livelihood with ease. They
agreed, and fixed for him a set salary of a thousand Reichstaler in specie a year, which is two thousand five hundred
Dutch gulden **[a]**.

Then he moved his home from Altona and came to Amsterdam with great honour — not only from the Ashkenazi community,
for the Sephardi community too showed him every honour, since he was very great in their eyes, and many of their
sages had known him for a long time, having sent him their letters and their questions. Just as he had been to the
Sephardi community in Hamburg a father and patron all the days he was there — they showed him great affection,
recognising his godly qualities, his wisdom and his study of Torah in truth and for the sake of Heaven. He also spoke
with them in their own language, a pure Spanish like theirs. So the head of the court of the Sephardi community of
Amsterdam in particular sought him out with a letter full of praise, which went ahead of him, to welcome him before
my father came to his dwelling in Amsterdam. When he came, the Sephardi community there too received him with honour
such as had not been shown to any man before him. They esteemed no rabbi or sage of all the communities of Germany
