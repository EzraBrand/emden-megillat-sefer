There in Altona the Blessed One built him "a sure house" (1 Sam. 2:35), and set up for him the great house of

Rashi and Tosafot, the codes, halakhot and aggadot. They also "filled their hands to the Lord" with other
knowledge, until they became perfect in Torah and in wisdom. Many times they completed with him the whole
Talmud and the *Ṭurim*, Bible and Midrash, and a book of grammar.

They also learned from him abstinence and caution, the way of true piety and inward fear of Heaven. For he
hated unjust gain, and despised even money that other rabbis counted as honest. Never in his life did he take
gifts of money from flesh and blood, from private persons, although many times the leaders of the people, the
rich men of his generation — men great and important like princes and dukes — wanted to make him rich. Many
times they brought him presents, precious vessels of silver and gold, and he sent them away empty-handed and
would take nothing from them.

Who can tell the greatness of his deeds? They would not be believed if they were told — except by one who saw
and knew and served him. Even now, at the time I write this scroll, there are still in the Three Communities
some old men who served him and remember his deeds, his holy and upright conduct and his teaching; they are
filled with joy and gladness when they speak of him. "To his name and to his memory is the desire of the soul"
(Isa. 26:8) of those who knew him of old, for none like him was heard or seen in these lands since ancient
years. Therefore he grew and prospered in the Torah and wisdom that he learned and taught; for all his Torah
was for the sake of Heaven, not to make himself great or to find favour in the eyes of men. All his aim and
desire was to increase the glory of his Creator and to raise up the pillars of the Torah, with all his heart,
all his soul and all his might.

He had the heart of a lion to rescue the oppressed from the oppressor; he showed no favour in matters of
Torah, and kept the command "You shall not be afraid of any man" (Deut. 1:17). Because of this trait some of the
powerful "rams" of the city were always opposed to him, as I will write, with God's help — not enemies of his
person, God forbid (except some worthless men, the insolent of the generation); for everyone knew and
recognised that he was a man of God, holy and faithful to God. For this very reason they feared him: because
he would not show them favour in anything that was not upright. There is no wonder in this. A man who would
not take from a human being even if they gave him all the silver, gold and pearls in the world will surely not
show partiality in judgement, and will do only what is right in the eyes of the Lord alone.

And the great wonder is that the lust for money and the love of silver, which "answers all things"
(Eccl. 10:19), could not turn his heart towards it, although he was burdened with sons and daughters, and his
house was empty of any large income. All the years he was rabbi in the Klaus he had only sixty Reichstaler a
year, a fixed sum, against very great and necessary expenses — in every

Above all, he was a very delicate and tender man, weak by nature and of a feeble constitution, so that he could

Once the famous and generous leader, the honoured R. Hirtz Hanover, of blessed memory **[a]**, happened to
come to Altona. He was exceedingly rich — worth many times a hundred thousand Reichstaler, as is known — and
with this he was very generous and free with his money in great abundance, and he loved worthy students of
Torah as himself. He happened to come to visit my father in his house, having heard of his greatness in Torah,
wisdom and fear of Heaven, and he stayed with him several hours and spoke with him about his business, to hear
his advice as well. In the end he asked him to agree to study one hour every day on his behalf, and he would
pay him for it. It would have cost him no trouble or toil and no loss of dignity at all, for in any case he was
diligent in Torah day and night without rest; and no one but he would know of it or check on him. Only one word
was lacking — to answer him, "Good, I will do so" — and then he would certainly have made him rich.

But hear now the answer; this is what he said to him: "Far be it from me to do this — to study for the sake of
human beings, to increase my reward, God forbid! I study Torah only for the love of the Creator: to know the
Torah, to keep it, to do and to fulfil it." The said leader wanted to give him a great gift, and he made his
word as nothing, and said to him: "Let your gifts be to yourself, and give your rewards to another"
(Dan. 5:17). And he went out from him disappointed, for he had not found what he sought from him: his intention
had been to bring an offering of first-fruits to so great a scholar.

He was the first in a matter of holiness, to give them the merit of wine for Kiddush and Havdalah. Before him

He enacted for them that food for the Sabbath be kept hot in closed ovens in the bakers' houses; until those
days they were lenient and kept food hot in embers in their houses, and stumbled into grave Sabbath
prohibitions.

Until his days they were careless in the preparation of matzot, so that they almost ate leaven on Passover. He
made a great enactment: to place at every ⟨S: "oven"⟩ where the Jews bake matzot a learned man, fit and
God-fearing, to supervise the making of the matzot in a kosher way, according to law and halakhah. And so that
poor and rich would be equal in this, without an undue burden on the poor man who cannot find workers, he
arranged to collect a small amount for every pound that was baked; only the rich man paid double. Even so it
came out cheaper for the rich than if they had hired workers on their own. With this money they hired common
workers to make matzot for all who came to bake, rich and poor together, and the workers took their wage from

He abolished the bad custom by which women delayed immersion for some months after their purification from
childbirth, and withheld their marital times, which causes great harm. He removed prohibitions of interest in
which many had stumbled until then.

He was a father to the needy and to every man in distress, and he got the rich used to giving much charity and
doing kindness, for his words were heeded — all the more since he did not seek his own honour, for the Lord was
with him, and he took no gift from private persons. And though the members of the communities were then at the
height of prosperity, as I have written, silver was almost of no account (1 Kgs. 10:21). Because they trusted
his righteousness and faithfulness, several rich men placed a certain sum in his hands, to distribute to poor
people of good family and to the upright in his eyes, so as to gain the great merit that is highest of the
levels of charity: the giver does not know to whom he gives, and the receiver does not know from whom he
receives. They also deposited ready money with him, to lend to the poor on pledges as a loan of grace and
kindness, as I can show by a receipt (*Quittung*).

His net was spread to Jerusalem too: his watchful eyes were upon them for good, that the money for the Land of
Israel collected in the Three Communities should reach the hands of the poor of Jerusalem, and be distributed
in order and honestly — not as in the other Ashkenazi communities, where all the money for the Land of Israel
went to waste. It was eaten by their general treasurers (the great thieves, who enrich themselves from this
charity money, while the poor of the Land of Israel grow poorer and are forced to leave their home in the Land
and become wanderers for bread, exiles and drifters), who were appointed over it in Frankfurt am Main, in

Even in the holy community of Altona, the leader R. Ber Kohen opposed him, so that this great thing should not
be carried out. He rose up as an adversary to this good matter, and withheld the good, so that they should not
have the merit of this great commandment. (And now his descendants wander for bread (Job 15:23); no one breaks
bread for them (Lam. 4:4), and there is no comforter.) Over this let all who grieve, grieve.

Let us return to the subject. "Lo, these are but the edges of his ways" (Job 26:14) — of the man of God, my
father, of blessed memory. To explain them all is impossible: many sheets would be needed, much ink would be
spilled and pens broken, and still they would not be enough to explain them, nor would they reach half of his
praise. Part of his praise you will find written down by me from my childhood; I set it in the eulogy that I
called *Yatziv Pitgam*.

(Prov. 22:29), and the Lord gave him favour in the eyes of the authorities wherever he stood, as I have written
a little of this, with God's help. He was also generous with his money and expert in worldly matters and in
political conduct, and they had the benefit of his counsel and wisdom. Above all, he was a man holy to his God,
a great pietist in all his ways and modest in all his affairs. He was

More of his godly traits: even if they had given him all the treasures of the world, he would not have gone back
on his word. No word of jest or levity was ever heard from his mouth, although he was of a cheerful mind and
spoke to every man with a pleasant face. With all this he was feared and held in awe, and loved and dear to all
who knew him; and those who knew him held him bound(?) to strengthened holiness and purity and an extra spirit:
little speech, little pleasure, little sleep (cf. Avot 6:6); full of the spirit of wisdom and fear of Heaven,
discipline and humility. He did not look beyond his four cubits. "His delight was in the Torah of the Lord"
all day and all night; in it he meditated, "in its love he was ever ravished" (Prov. 5:19). And though "the
Torah made him hot" (b. Ta'anit 4a), still he was accepted by the people close to him, who knew his ways,
because they knew that all his deeds were for the sake of Heaven, without any outward motive.

Therefore his friends and disciples devoted themselves to him: they cared for him and provided him with a
livelihood with honour, so that he was not forced to leave the trait of piety that he had held from his youth,
not to accept a gift. He did not enjoy an optional banquet; and they could not move him against his will to
